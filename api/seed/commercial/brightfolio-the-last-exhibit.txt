ONE

Nobody at the Hollis Museum noticed the painting was wrong until a nine-year-old said so.

It was a Tuesday in October, the slow part of the afternoon when the light through the clerestory windows goes the colour of weak tea, and Priya Raghunathan was doing what she did most Tuesdays: standing in Gallery Four with a clipboard, counting visitors for a report nobody read.

The girl was with a school group. She had detached herself from it in the way children do, drifting backwards until the teacher's voice became somebody else's problem, and she had ended up in front of the Vermeer.

It was not a Vermeer. It was *attributed to* Vermeer, which in the trade means somebody hopes very much, and the Hollis had been hoping since 1962. *Woman at a Casement* — a figure half-turned at a window, a letter in her left hand, the famous grey-blue light doing what that light does.

"That's wrong," the girl said.

Priya looked up from her clipboard. "What's wrong?"

"The window." The girl pointed, not quite touching the rope. "The light's coming in from there." She moved her finger. "But her shadow's going that way. It should be the other way. Like —" She turned side-on and held up her own arm to demonstrate, frowning at the floor to check her working.

Priya looked at the painting.

She had looked at this painting perhaps four thousand times. She had written a chapter of her thesis on it. She had stood in front of it with donors and schoolchildren and a Dutch specialist who had flown in from Delft to be politely non-committal for ninety minutes, and in all that time she had never once looked at the shadow under the woman's left hand, because it was small and dark and in the part of the canvas where your eye does not go.

The girl was right. The shadow fell towards the window.

"Huh," said Priya.

"Is it a mistake?"

"I don't know." It came out more honestly than she intended. "I genuinely don't know. That's — that's quite unusual, for me to not know about this painting."

The girl looked pleased in a guarded way, as if expecting the adult to take it back.

Then the teacher called, and the group moved on towards the Impressionists, and Priya stood alone in Gallery Four with a clipboard she had stopped writing on.

TWO

The thing about an attribution is that it is not a fact. It is a sentence that a sufficient number of serious people have agreed to keep saying.

Priya knew this better than most. She had spent four years of her twenties watching a Rembrandt become a not-Rembrandt — slowly, through a committee, with enormous courtesy — and she had learned that the moment of change is never a discovery. It is a consensus shifting its weight from one foot to the other.

*Woman at a Casement* had been bought by Hollis money in 1962 for a sum that the museum still did not discuss. It was on the posters. It was on the mug in Priya's kitchen. It was the reason the Hollis appeared in survey books at all, and everyone who worked there understood, without ever having been told, that the painting was not really a painting; it was the foundation the building stood on.

She stayed until nine that night with a raking light and a loupe.

By seven she had found the second thing: a passage of the sleeve where the brushwork changed character, became tighter, more anxious, a different hand or the same hand on a different day. By half past eight she had found the third, which was worse, because it was not an error at all. It was a correction. Someone had painted out a second figure in the doorway — you could see the ghost of it under the raking light, a shoulder and part of a head, sunk beneath three hundred years of varnish.

A correction meant someone cared enough to change their mind. That was the kind of detail that made a painting more real, not less.

So now she had a canvas with an impossible shadow, an inconsistent sleeve, and a pentimento that argued for exactly the sort of working process the attribution depended on — three facts that refused to resolve into a single story.

Priya sat down on the gallery floor, which staff were not supposed to do, and looked up at the woman at the window.

"What are you?" she said.

The woman went on reading her letter, in light that came from the wrong direction, the way she had for three hundred and some years, declining to say.

THREE

She told Gerald Okonkwo-Price first, because he was the registrar and because he was the only person at the Hollis who had never once lied to her about money.

He listened to the whole thing without interrupting, which took eleven minutes, and then sat back and put both hands flat on the desk.

"Who else knows?"

"Nobody. A nine-year-old."

"A nine-year-old." He closed his eyes briefly. "Priya. Do you know what the insurance revaluation is scheduled for?"

"March."

"March." He opened his eyes. "And you understand that the moment you put this in writing — not publish, not announce, *write it down in an email* — it becomes a thing the museum knew. There is no version of this where we look at it quietly for a year and then decide."

"I know."

"Do you want me to tell you not to?"

Priya thought about it properly, because he had asked properly.

"No," she said. "I want you to tell me what it costs."

Gerald reached for a legal pad, and began, in his small precise hand, to write a list.
