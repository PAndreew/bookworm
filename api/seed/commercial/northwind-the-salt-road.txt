CHAPTER ONE

The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter

The lamp failed at 3:14 in the morning, and Mara knew it the way you know your own heartbeat stopping.

She had slept beside that light for nineteen years. Its turn was in her bones — eight seconds of sweep, two of dark, eight of sweep — and when the rhythm broke she was already out of bed and halfway up the spiral before she was properly awake, her feet finding the worn places in the stone without being asked.

The lamp room was cold. That was the first wrong thing. The mantle should have been throwing heat enough to make the glass sweat, and instead the air had the flat chill of a room that had been empty for hours.

The second wrong thing was her father, sitting in the keeper's chair with his back to the dead light, facing the sea.

"Da." She said it quietly, the way you speak to someone standing at an edge. "Da, the lamp."

"I know."

"There's a boat out there. The Hendry boy said he saw running lights off the point before the weather came in."

"I know that too."

He did not turn around. In the grey coming through the glass she could see the shape of him, the particular slope of his shoulders that she had been reading since she was small enough to be carried up these stairs. It was the slope that meant a decision had already been made and she was merely the last to hear it.

Mara crossed to the lamp and put her hand on the brass housing. Stone cold.

"How long?" she said.

"Since midnight."

Three hours. Three hours of the Skerries unlit, in weather that had been building since Tuesday, with at least one boat somewhere out past the point trying to find the channel by memory and the grace of God.

She found she was very calm. That surprised her. She had expected, if this moment ever came, to feel something enormous — and instead there was only a kind of terrible practicality, a list forming itself in her head. Oil. Wick. Matches. The spare mantle in the cupboard under the stairs that her father thought she did not know about.

"Right," she said, and started moving.

"Mara."

"Don't."

"They're going to take the light anyway. The Commission's decided. Automatic by spring, and us out of here by Michaelmas, and no appeal because there's no one left to appeal to." His voice was perfectly level, which was how she knew how bad it was. "I've kept it forty-one years. I thought — if it fails on its own, if it just fails, then it's the light that's finished and not me."

She stopped with her hand on the cupboard door.

"And the boat?"

A long silence. The wind found a gap somewhere in the glazing and made a sound like someone drawing breath to speak.

"I've been sitting here three hours," her father said, "trying to want it to go down."

Mara opened the cupboard. The spare mantle was there, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where it had been since she was eleven years old and had worked out that her father hid things he was frightened of needing.

"Then it's lucky," she said, "that you've got me."

CHAPTER TWO

What the Tide Brought

The light came back at 4:02.

Mara did it alone, not because her father refused to help but because he had gone very still in the chair and she understood, with the clarity that comes at four in the morning, that if she asked him for anything at all he would break in a way that could not be mended. So she did it herself: cleaned the burner, fitted the mantle with hands that shook only at the very end, and touched the match to it with her eyes shut, because she could not bear to watch if it failed.

It did not fail. The light caught, hesitated, and then came up hard and white, and the great lens picked it up and threw it out across eleven miles of black water, and the rhythm started again as if it had never stopped. Eight seconds of sweep. Two of dark.

She stood in the middle of it and let it go round and round her, and only then did she let herself cry — briefly, furiously, the way you do when you have won something you had no business winning.

The boat came in at half past six.

It was a thirty-foot inshore trawler, the *Ellen Rose* out of Portnahaven, and it came round the point under a sky the colour of a bruise with its wheelhouse window stove in and one man at the helm who had been awake for thirty-one hours. Mara watched it from the gallery with her father's glass, and she saw the exact moment the helmsman found the light — saw the boat's head come round, hunting, and then steady, and then hold, straight as a ruled line, into the channel.

She went down to the jetty to meet them.

There were four men aboard and one of them was a boy younger than she was, wrapped in somebody else's coat and shaking so hard he could not hold the cup she put in his hands. The helmsman was the last one off. He stood on the stones for a moment, looking back at the lighthouse with an expression she could not read at all.

"Three hours," he said. "Three hours with no light. We were running on the compass and a chart from 1931."

"I know."

"Then it came back." He looked at her. He had a face like weather, all lines and salt, and his eyes were red-rimmed and entirely steady. "I've been at this thirty years and I have never been so glad to see anything in my life. Whoever lit that lamp —"

Mara did not say anything.

Behind her, on the path up from the jetty, she heard her father's step on the gravel. She did not turn round. She had the strangest sense of standing at a hinge in her own life, the sort of moment you only recognise afterwards, and she thought: whatever I say now, I will be living with for a very long time.

"It was the keeper," she said.

And that was true, and it was also a lie, and she was not sure yet which part of it mattered more.
