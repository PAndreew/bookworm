PART ONE: NOVEMBER

One

The glasshouse had been dying for six years before anyone was willing to use the word.

Tomas learned this the way he learned most things about Fenwick Park — sideways, from a man who did not realise he was explaining anything. The man was Len, who had been head gardener for twenty-two years and who had the particular stillness of someone who has stopped expecting to be consulted.

"You'll want to mind the fourth bay," Len said, on Tomas's first morning. "Don't stand under it long."

"Is it unsafe?"

Len considered this for what seemed like a very long time.

"It's honest," he said. "That's different."

The glasshouse at Fenwick was four hundred feet of Victorian iron and glass, built in 1871 by a cotton man who wanted to grow pineapples in Lancashire and very nearly managed it. It ran the length of the walled garden in three great barrel vaults, and in photographs from the 1930s it looked like something out of a dream — all that light, and inside it a jungle, palms pressed against the panes like hands.

Now it held forty-one surviving plants, a standing puddle that never quite dried, and a smell Tomas could not identify for his first fortnight and then could never stop noticing: wet iron and old soil and something sweetly rotten underneath.

He had been hired to assess it. That was the word in the contract — *assess* — and he understood within about four days that the word had been chosen with great care by people who did not want to say *condemn*.

Two

The Trust had three options and no money, which is a way of saying it had one option and a lot of meetings.

Tomas sat through the first of these in a room above the stable block, with a radiator that clanked and seven people who had known each other for decades. He had prepared a structural summary. He got as far as the second page.

"I'm sorry," said a woman named Dorothy Achebe-Lindqvist, who chaired the friends' group and who had, Len told him later, personally raised eleven thousand pounds for the glasshouse roof in 1998. "Can you go back? You said the fourth bay was beyond economic repair."

"I did."

"The fourth bay is the vine house."

"Yes."

"The vine is a hundred and fifty years old."

Tomas had known this was coming since Tuesday, and he had rehearsed several answers, and in the event he used none of them.

"I know," he said. "I've been sitting under it for a fortnight trying to find a way to write this report that doesn't end where it ends. The iron in that bay has lost about forty per cent of its section to corrosion. It's holding up two tonnes of glass. It is holding it up today. I can't tell you it will be holding it up next winter, and I'm not willing to write a sentence that implies I can."

The radiator clanked.

"And the vine?" Dorothy said.

"The vine will not survive being moved. I'm sorry. I've asked three people who know more about vines than I do, and they all said the same thing in slightly different words."

Nobody spoke for a while.

It was not, Tomas thought, the silence of people disagreeing with him. It was the silence of people doing arithmetic they had been avoiding for six years, and arriving, one by one, at the same figure.

Three

Len found him afterwards, out by the cold frames, in the dark.

"You said it straight," Len said. It was not obviously a compliment.

"It's what they're paying me for."

"No." Len lit a cigarette with the unhurried competence of a man who has given up four times. "They're paying you to say it so they don't have to. That's a different job. Costs more."

They stood there. Through the glass, Tomas could see the shape of the vine — the main stem thick as his thigh, going up into the dark and then running away along the ridge for ninety feet, the way it had since before anyone now living was born.

"Will they fight it?" Tomas asked.

"Dorothy will. Not because she thinks you're wrong." Len drew on the cigarette. "Because somebody ought to, and she's the only one of them with the stomach for it. She'll fight it for about four months and then she'll stop, and the stopping will take more out of her than the fighting did."

"That's bleak."

"That's November." Len looked at him sideways, and something in his face shifted — not warmth exactly, but the opening of a door that had been shut. "Come back in March, though. Before you file the final thing. Come and see it in March."

"Why?"

"Because in March that vine will come into leaf," Len said, "and it will do it whether or not we've decided to let it. And you should know what you're writing about."
