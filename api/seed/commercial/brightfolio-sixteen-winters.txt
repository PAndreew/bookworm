PROLOGUE

My grandmother kept a list of everyone who had ever lent her money, and she kept it for sixty-three years, and she paid all of them back.

The list is in front of me now. It is a school exercise book, the kind with a pale green cover and a multiplication table printed inside the back, and the first entry is dated March 1951 and reads: *Mrs Kowalczyk — 2/6 — bus fare + bread.*

Two shillings and sixpence. She was nineteen, four months off the boat, working nights at a laundry in Salford and sharing a room with two other girls who were also nineteen and also four months off a boat. She had no English beyond what she had taught herself from a phrasebook on the crossing, and she had the exercise book, which she had bought with money she did not really have, because her father had told her once that a person who loses track of what they owe has already begun to become someone else.

The last entry is dated 2014. By then she was eighty-two, she owned the laundry, and the amount was four hundred pounds — lent to her, for reasons the book does not explain, by a man named Dennis.

Between those two entries are three hundred and eleven lines.

I have been trying to write about my grandmother for eleven years. Every version failed in the same place and for the same reason, which is that I kept trying to make her admirable, and she was not admirable. She was formidable, which is a different thing and much harder to love.

This book is the version where I stopped doing that.

ONE

She did not talk about the crossing, and for most of my childhood I assumed this was because it had been terrible.

It had been terrible. But that was not why.

I found out at her kitchen table when I was twenty-six, during the only genuinely honest conversation we ever had, which happened because I had just been made redundant and arrived at her house at four in the afternoon on a Wednesday with nowhere else to be. She made tea. She did not ask what had happened. She sat down opposite me and said, apropos of nothing:

"You know I was not supposed to come."

"What?"

"On the boat. The place was not mine." She turned her cup a quarter turn, which was a thing she did. "It was my cousin Irena's. She was ill and she could not travel and the ticket was paid, and my father said, you go, and I went."

I waited.

"That is all," she said.

"That's not all. What happened to Irena?"

"She got better. She stayed. She married a man from the next village and she had four children and she died in 1997 and I never saw her again after I was eighteen years old." My grandmother said this in exactly the voice she used for the price of fish. "I wrote to her every month for forty-six years. She wrote back. We never once mentioned the ticket."

I remember the kitchen very clearly. The clock. The particular yellow of the light.

"Were you ashamed?" I asked, because I was twenty-six and I thought that was a question you could ask.

She looked at me then with an expression I had never seen on her face, and have thought about every week since.

"No," she said. "That is the thing you will not understand, and I am too old to explain it properly. I was not ashamed. I was *owing*. It is not the same. Shame you carry and it makes you small. Owing you carry and it makes you —" She stopped, and did the quarter turn with the cup again, hunting for a word in a language she had learned at nineteen out of a phrasebook. "It makes you keep going. Because you cannot stop while it is still outstanding."

"Grandma, you can't pay back a boat ticket from 1951."

"No," she agreed. "You cannot."

And she got up and refilled the pot, and that was the end of it, and I sat there with the beginnings of an understanding that has taken me the rest of my adult life to properly arrive at.

TWO

Here is the thing I could not write for eleven years.

The exercise book is not a record of debts. It is a record of a woman building, deliberately and over six decades, a structure of obligation large enough to hold the weight of one unpayable thing.

Every two-and-six she wrote down and paid back was a small, closable version of the big one. Every name she crossed off was proof — to herself, in her own handwriting — that she was a person who settled accounts. She could not settle the account with Irena. So she settled three hundred and eleven others, and she did it with a thoroughness that her family found exhausting and occasionally insulting, because she could not be given anything. Not a lift to the station. Not a birthday lunch. Everything was reciprocated within the month, and always slightly over, and we all thought it was pride.

It was not pride. It was arithmetic, carried out in the dark, by a nineteen-year-old who had taken her cousin's place on a boat.

I do not know what to do with this. I am not sure it is a lesson. I have spent my thirties noticing the same shape in myself — the compulsive squaring-up, the inability to receive — and I have not managed to stop, and I am no longer certain I want to.

What I have instead is the exercise book, and three hundred and eleven names, and the particular yellow of the light in that kitchen.
